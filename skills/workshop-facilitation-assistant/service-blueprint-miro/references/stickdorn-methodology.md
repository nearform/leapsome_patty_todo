# Stickdorn's Service Design Methodology

This reference contains Marc Stickdorn's complete service design methodology for creating effective service blueprints.

## The Six Core Principles

### 1. Human-Centred
Consider the experience of ALL people affected by the service:
- End customers (consumers, shoppers)
- Primary customers (business buyers, managers)
- Employees (sales reps, support staff, operations)
- Partners (suppliers, third-party vendors)
- Indirect stakeholders (family members of employees, community impact)

**In practice**: When mapping a B2B platform, include end users, business decision-makers, internal staff, account managers, operations teams, and system administrators.

### 2. Collaborative
Service design cannot be done FOR users - it must be done WITH users, customers, and employees.

**In practice**:
- Include knowledge holders from each swimlane in blueprint creation
- Cross-departmental involvement - broader scope requires more departments
- Treat the blueprint as a checklist ensuring all relevant parties contribute
- Run co-creation workshops, not solo design sessions

### 3. Iterative
An exploratory, adaptive approach iterating toward implementation through cycles of research, ideation, prototyping.

**In practice**:
- Create draft blueprints, test with stakeholders, refine
- Start with rough sketches on whiteboards, then digitise
- Use "sh*tty first drafts" approach - make failure acceptable
- Set up regular review cycles (weekly, bi-weekly)
- Update blueprints as services evolve

### 4. Sequential
Visualise and orchestrate the service as a sequence of interrelated actions over time.

**Key distinction**: Process maps have branches and decision trees. Journey maps and blueprints maintain **one linear story of one person** to preserve the narrative thread essential for empathy.

**In practice**:
- Tell one persona's story from start to finish
- Use time as horizontal axis (left to right flow)
- If multiple paths exist, create separate blueprints for each
- Avoid "if/then" logic within a single blueprint

### 5. Real
Research in reality, prototype in reality, evidence intangible values as physical or digital reality.

**In practice**:
- Base blueprints on actual observed user sessions
- Include real pain points from customer interviews
- Reference actual system performance data
- Test prototypes with real users in real contexts
- Use theatrical prototyping (investigative rehearsal, desktop walkthrough)

### 6. Holistic
Address the needs of all stakeholders through the entire service and across the business.

**In practice**:
- Map entire end-to-end journey (not just the part your team owns)
- Include all supporting systems and third parties
- Consider employee experience alongside customer experience
- Connect front office to back office operations

## Service Design as Five Lenses

Stickdorn views service design through five complementary perspectives:

1. **Mindset** - Open, empathetic, experimental approach to problem-solving
2. **Process** - Iterative cycles through research, ideation, prototyping, implementation
3. **Toolset** - Journey maps, blueprints, personas, stakeholder maps
4. **Language** - Visual communication that breaks down silos between departments
5. **Management** - Human-centric KPIs, innovation governance, organisational change

## Service Blueprint vs Journey Map vs Process Map

| Tool | Purpose | Focus | Structure |
|------|---------|-------|-----------|
| **Journey Map** | Understand customer experience | Customer actions, emotions, touchpoints | Linear narrative, customer perspective only |
| **Service Blueprint** | Connect customer experience to operations | Customer + frontstage + backstage + systems | Linear narrative with operational layers |
| **Process Map** | Optimise internal efficiency | Business processes, decision logic, workflows | Branches, decision trees, flowchart logic |

**Key insight**: Service blueprints are a hybrid - they maintain the linear empathy-building narrative of journey maps while adding the operational depth of process maps.

## Managing Complexity: The Pyramid Approach

Rather than one overwhelming comprehensive blueprint, build a **hierarchy of linked maps at different zoom levels**:

### Level 1: Lifecycle Overview (Highest Level)
- **Scope**: Entire customer relationship
- **Stages**: 4-7 major phases (e.g., Onboarding → Regular Usage → Expansion → Renewal)
- **Detail**: High-level activities only
- **Purpose**: Executive communication, strategic alignment

### Level 2: Stage Detail (Medium Level)
- **Scope**: One specific stage from Level 1
- **Stages**: 5-8 sub-stages
- **Detail**: 20-25 customer actions total
- **Purpose**: Operational design, identifying improvements

### Level 3: Process Flow (Lowest Level)
- **Scope**: One specific subprocess
- **Stages**: Granular steps
- **Detail**: Technical implementation, system interactions
- **Purpose**: Development specifications, technical design

**Navigation**: Use unique URLs or Miro board links to connect levels. Always be able to "zoom in" from overview to detail and "zoom out" from detail to context.

## The 20-25 Activity Rule

**Target**: 20-25 customer activities per individual blueprint

**Why this matters**:
- Fewer than 15 activities: Probably missing detail or scope too narrow
- More than 30 activities: Overwhelming to stakeholders, too much to process
- 20-25 sweet spot: Comprehensive yet digestible

**If you have more**: Split into multiple blueprints at different stages or create hierarchical levels.

## Waiting Time: The Forgotten Customer Action

**Critical**: Always include WAITING TIME as explicit customer actions.

"Very often, if you focus on the internal processes, you simply forget that people have to wait while you're doing something."

**Examples of waiting time**:
- "Waits for page to load"
- "Waits for approval"
- "Waits for confirmation email"
- "Waits for delivery window"
- "Waits on hold for customer service"

**Why it matters**: Waiting time is often where customer frustration peaks, but organisations ignore it because they're busy during those periods.

## Emotional Journey Integration

Unlike traditional Shostack blueprints, Stickdorn integrates emotional data throughout:

### Methods to Capture Emotions:

1. **Emotional curve/dramatic arc**: Line graph showing satisfaction over time
2. **Emoji indicators**: 😊 😐 😟 😤 for quick visual scanning
3. **Text descriptions**: "Frustrated by slow loading" or "Relieved order confirmed"
4. **Colour coding**: Green (positive), yellow (neutral), red (negative)

### Key Moments to Flag:

- **Peak moments**: Highest satisfaction or engagement points
- **Pain points**: Moments of frustration, confusion, or failure
- **Moments of truth**: Critical interactions that define service perception
- **Breaking points**: Where customers might abandon the service

## The Line of Visibility Philosophy

**Line of Interaction**: Separates what customer does from what system/employee does
**Line of Visibility**: Separates what customer sees from what happens behind the scenes
**Line of Internal Interaction**: Separates employee actions from supporting systems

**Stickdorn's perspective**: These lines are guidelines, not rigid rules. Adapt based on your service's complexity. Some blueprints might need additional dividing lines (e.g., Line of Digital Interaction for omnichannel services).

## Workshop Best Practices

### Preparation (Most Important Phase)
- Spend significant time on preparation (Stickdorn recommends 2-3x the workshop duration)
- Pre-populate blueprints with research findings before the workshop
- Have draft personas and journey maps ready as inputs
- Test all technology and templates beforehand

### Warm-ups (Essential for Energy)
- Always start with warm-ups - get people to laugh together
- Recommended: "Three-brain warm-up", "Color-chain", "Yes, and..." improv
- Warm-ups break down hierarchies and create psychological safety

### Timing Techniques
- **Liquid timing**: Use odd numbers (7, 16, 19 minutes) not rounded numbers (5, 10, 15)
- Rounded numbers make people relax and procrastinate
- Hide the timer - "Time is the tool for the facilitator, not for participants"

### Group Size Variations
- **Individual work** (many pens, many pages): Divergent thinking, speed
- **Parallel contribution** (many pens, one page): Shared canvas, diverse input
- **Sequential contribution** (one pen, one page): Consensus, completeness

### Quality Outputs
- Be extremely specific with task instructions
- Use large templates - "If templates are too small, it constrains people"
- Ask participants to sketch during journey mapping (visualisation increases understanding)
- Always work with real users when possible

### Dramatic Arc ("Boom-Wow-Wow-Wow-Boom")
Design workshop experiences like James Bond movies:
- Start with high energy (warm-up)
- Create peaks and valleys
- End with high energy (action planning)
- Never flat, monotonous sessions

## Miro vs Smaply: When to Use Each

**Use Miro for**:
- Co-creative workshop flexibility
- Remote collaboration with diverse stakeholders
- Rapid iteration and sketching
- One-off blueprints or discovery work
- When you need freeform canvas capabilities

**Use Smaply for**:
- Standardising maps across organisation
- Scaling service design practice
- Creating navigable hierarchies of linked maps
- Long-term blueprint maintenance
- When templates and consistency matter

**Stickdorn quote**: "If you run a remote co-creative workshop, I do not recommend Smaply because it's not made for this... I recommend Smaply when it's about standardizing maps and when you want to scale service design."

## Maintenance and Governance

Blueprints are living documents. Set up:

1. **Ownership**: Assign one person/team responsible for each blueprint
2. **Update schedule**: Regular reviews (monthly, quarterly)
3. **Version control**: Track changes over time
4. **Access**: Make blueprints easily discoverable and accessible
5. **Integration**: Connect blueprints to OKRs, roadmaps, project backlogs

**Warning signs of neglect**:
- Blueprint hasn't been updated in 6+ months
- References outdated systems or processes
- No one can explain what blueprint shows
- Stored in inaccessible location (someone's hard drive)
